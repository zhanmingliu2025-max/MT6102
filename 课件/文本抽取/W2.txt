[p18] 2.1 Mercantilism | • A country’s wealth determined by amount of its gold  | and silver holdings.  | • Mercantilists believed that a country should increase  | its holdings of gold and silver by promoting exports  | and discouraging imports.

[p20] 2.2 Absolute Advantage | • Ability of a nation to produce a good more efficiently  | than any other nation (greater output using same or  | fewer resources).
[p21] 2.2 Absolute Advantage | Examples | 1. Easier to extract oil in Saudi Arabia than in any other  | country. Abundance of oil makes it easier to drill for  | oil whereas other countries involves exploration and  | drilling cost.  | 2. Columbia has climatic advantage of producing coffee.
[p22] 2.2 Absolute Advantage | • Trade shouldn’t be regulated by government policy or  | intervention – determined by market forces. | • By specialization, countries generate efficiencies  | because their labour force would become more skilled  | by doing the same tasks.  | • A nation’s wealth shouldn’t be judged by amount of  | gold/silver but rather the living standards of its people.

[p27] 2.4 Heckscher-Ohlin | • Focused their attention on how a country could gain  | comparative advantage by producing products that  | utilized factors that were in abundance in the country. | • Factors (e.g., land, labour and capital) that were in  | great supply relative to demand would be cheaper.  |   | E.g., China and India – optimal locations for labour- |   | intensive industries like garments.
[p28] 2.5 Country Similarity  | • Countries tend to trade more with other countries that  | are similar in terms of culture, language, geography  | and economic development.  | • Countries with similarities more likely to have similar  | demand patterns and preferences for goods and  | services. | • Criticised for oversimplifying complex factors that  | influence international trade patterns.
[p29] 2.6 Product Life Cycle | • A product goes through three  | stages: | • During _____________, product is  | produced in the country where it  | was invented. | • As product _____, production shifts  | to countries with lower production  | costs. | • At _______ stage, production shifts  | to countries where product can be  | produced most efficiently.
[p30] 2.7 Global Strategic Rivalry | • Focuses on MNCs and their  | ability to gain competitive  | advantage against other  | global firms in their industry. | • Critical ways that firms can  | obtain sustainable  | competitive advantages are  | called barriers to entry for  | that industry.
[p31] 2.8 Porter’s National Competitive Advantage | • A nation’s competitiveness  | in an industry depends on  | the capacity of the industry  | to innovate and upgrade.  | • Porter’s theory focused on  | explaining why some nations  | are more competitive in  | certain industries.
[p32] 2.8 Porter’s National Competitive Advantage | # Local market resources and capabilities (factor  | conditions) | • A nation’s resources (e.g. natural resources and  | available labour) as key factors in determining what  | products a country will import or export.  | • Other advanced factors include – skilled labour,  | investments in education, technology and  | infrastructure.
[p33] 2.8 Porter’s National Competitive Advantage | # Local market demand conditions | • A sophisticated home market critical to ensuring  | ongoing innovation > creating sustainable competitive  | advantage.  |   | E.g. Silicon Valley in US – global centre of  | technological innovation.
[p34] 2.8 Porter’s National Competitive Advantage | #Local suppliers and complementary industries | • To remain competitive, large global firms benefit from  | strong, efficient supporting and related industries.  | #Local firm characteristics | • Include firm strategy, industry structure, and industry  | rivalry.  | • Healthy rivalry between local firms will spur innovation  | and competitiveness.
